# How the camera decides

You never place a camera in BloomStudio. You say what kind of shot you want and
what it is looking at, and the studio works out where the camera goes.

This is not a simplification for beginners — it is how the shot survives editing.
If you move a character after placing a camera by hand, the camera is now wrong.
If the shot is "close-up on Buzzy", it is still a close-up on Buzzy.

## The shot sizes

| Framing | Shows | Measured from |
|---|---|---|
| Wide | The whole place | Body height |
| Full | Head to toe | Body height |
| Medium | Waist up | Head size |
| Close-up | Head and shoulders | Head size |
| Extreme close-up | The face | Head size |
| Over-shoulder | Past one character to another | Head size |
| Two-shot | Everybody in the scene | The group |
| Low angle | Looking up — makes them look big | Body height |
| High angle | Looking down — makes them look small | Body height |

**Why some are measured from the head.** These characters are stylised: a bee's
head is a third of its height, a child's is nearly a quarter. "Show the top 40%
of the body" gives a portrait on a realistic figure and an extreme close-up on a
cartoon one. Tight framings are therefore specified in head-diameters, which is
what "head and shoulders" actually means.

**Two-shots measure the group.** A two-shot finds every visible character,
measures from the lowest pair of feet to the highest head, and widens until they
all fit with a margin. Framing on one of them and hoping is how you get a video
full of half-visible characters.

## The moves

- **Push in** — slowly closer. Use it on the line that matters. One per scene.
- **Pull out** — slowly wider. Reveals where you are; good at the end of a scene.
- **Pan left / right** — sweeps across. Good for showing a row of things.
- **Orbit** — arcs around the subject. Striking, and tiring at any length.
- **Crane up** — rises and tilts down. An ending.
- **Handheld** — a small, natural drift. Adds life to a static conversation.
- **Follow** — tracks a character who is moving.

Move amount scales all of them. Below about 0.4 a move reads as a mistake rather
than a choice; above 1.2 it starts to be about itself.

## What the automatic cut does

When you build from a script, the studio cuts as follows:

1. Every scene opens on a **wide** shot, held for about a second, pushing gently
   in. The audience needs to know where they are before they are asked to look
   at a face.
2. It cuts when the **speaker changes**, or when a shot has run longer than
   about six and a half seconds.
3. Framings **cycle** — medium, close-up, two-shot, full, close-up — so a long
   conversation keeps changing shape.
4. Lines longer than four seconds get a **push-in**; every fourth shot gets a
   gentle **handheld**.
5. Audience beats always cut to a **close-up** of whoever asked, and the
   character's eyes go to the lens.

The result is roughly a cut every four seconds, which is about right for
preschool. It is a starting point: every shot is editable, and the ones worth
changing are usually the ones where the automatic choice put the camera on the
wrong character.

## Making a shot better by hand

The two things that most often need fixing:

**The camera is on the wrong person.** The automatic cut follows the speaker.
Sometimes the interesting thing is the *reaction* — Dotty's face when she bites
the lemon matters more than Buzzy explaining it. Change "Points at".

**The shot is too long.** If a shot outlives its idea, the audience notices the
shot instead of the story. Drag its right edge in; everything after slides along.
