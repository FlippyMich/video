# Making a video children actually watch

This is the part no tool can do for you. It is also the part that decides
whether the video works, so it is worth more than every setting in the studio
put together.

## Repetition is the content

Adults experience repetition as padding. Three- to six-year-olds experience it as
mastery. A structure that feels obvious to you — *name the thing, try the thing,
name it again, count it* — is what makes the idea stick, and a child who can
predict what comes next is a child who joins in.

*Buzzy's Sense-ational Adventure!* uses the same five-beat shape for every sense:

1. Announce it. "Sense number one."
2. Do something with it. Cover your eyes, then open them.
3. Name it. "That's your sense of SIGHT."
4. Practise it. Find something red. Now yellow. Now green.
5. Count it. "Hold up one finger."

Five times, identically. By the third sense the audience is running ahead of the
video, which is exactly what you want.

## Ask, then actually stop

The single most common mistake in children's video is asking a question and
answering it half a second later. If the pause is not long enough to answer in,
the audience learns within about ninety seconds that the questions are
rhetorical, and stops trying.

Rules of thumb:

- A one-word answer they already know: **3 seconds**.
- Something they have to look for: **4 seconds**.
- Singing or counting along: **5–6 seconds**.
- Never fill the pause with music that resolves. Let it sit.

And point the character's eyes at the lens. Direct address is what turns a
broadcast into a conversation — it is why every long-running preschool show does
it and why the studio does it automatically on every audience beat.

## Change something every ten to fifteen seconds

Not *cut* every ten seconds — change. A new shot, a new character, a prop
appearing, a sound, a colour. Attention in this age group is renewed by novelty,
and a scene that looks the same for thirty seconds has lost most of the room
regardless of what is being said.

The automatic cut in this studio averages a shot every four seconds, which
handles the visual half. The other half is the writing: bring somebody new in,
hand somebody an object, move outdoors.

## Say less

Compare:

> "Your tongue is a very clever part of your body, and it can tell you whether
> something is sweet or salty or sour, which is called taste."

with:

> "Sense number four. TASTING. Your tongue does all the work."

The second is not dumbed down; it is *finished*. Long sentences are not harder
to understand because of vocabulary, they are harder because a child has to hold
the beginning in mind while you get to the end.

One idea per line. One clause where you can manage it.

## One gesture per line

A character doing something on every word reads as noise. The studio deliberately
adds at most one gesture per line of dialogue, chosen to match the words —
pointing on "look", cupping an ear on "listen", leaning in on "smell".

If you add more by hand, take one away.

## Let the comedy be too big

Dotty biting the lemon is the biggest moment in the film. In a video for adults
you would undersell it. Here, oversell it: bigger reaction, longer hold, a sound
effect that is frankly ridiculous. Preschool comedy is physical and it is
generous, and playing it small reads as nothing happening.

## Length

Five to eight minutes, if something changes often enough. Beyond about eight,
completion rates fall off a cliff regardless of quality — better to make two
videos than one long one.

A useful test: write down what happens every thirty seconds. If any row is
empty, that is where you will lose them.

## And the thing that matters most

Children can tell whether the person making the video was enjoying themselves.
Whatever else you do, put the joke in.
